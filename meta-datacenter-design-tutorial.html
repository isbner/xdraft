1. Why study Meta's data centers?

Meta (formerly Facebook) operates one of the largest data center fleets on Earth, serving 3.4+ billion people. Its engineering blog is unusually open: the company publishes deep technical details about network topologies, switch hardware, optics, and software, and contributes most designs to the Open Compute Project (OCP). Studying their architecture is a masterclass in how to scale infrastructure when off-the-shelf solutions run out of headroom.

The central theme Every major redesign at Meta is driven by the same tension: exponential traffic growth colliding with hard physical limits — power, cooling, optics availability, and port density. The solution is almost always the same recipe: disaggregation + uniformity + software control.

2. Before 2014: The cluster era

Meta's earliest data centers used a classic hierarchical design:

  • Servers were grouped into racks with a Top-of-Rack (TOR) switch.
  • Racks were grouped into clusters — hundreds of racks aggregated by a few huge, high-radix "cluster switches" (the "four-post" architecture, 3+1 redundancy).
  • Clusters connected to each other over an oversubscribed uplink.

This worked until it didn't. The problems:

  • Vendor lock-in: the biggest clusters needed the biggest proprietary chassis switches, available from only a couple of vendors.
  • Blast radius: one chassis failure could take down a huge chunk of the data center.
  • Rigid capacity: inter-cluster bandwidth was oversubscribed by design, which assumed most traffic stayed inside a cluster — increasingly false as Meta's services became more distributed.
  • Slow speed upgrades: high-density boxes lagged in adopting the next Ethernet speed.
Key insight The "cluster" was a networking limitation masquerading as an architecture. Meta's applications scale by being distributed; the network was artificially confining them.

3. 2014: The Data Center Fabric

Era 1 · Disaggregation begins

In November 2014, Facebook unveiled the data center fabric, first deployed in the Altoona, Iowa data center. The goal: make the entire building one high-performance network, not a hierarchy of oversubscribed clusters.

3.1 The building blocks

Pod

The new atomic "unit of network." Each pod = 48 server racks, always the same size, served by 4 fabric switches. Small enough to use mid-size, multi-vendor switches. Each TOR had 4 × 40G uplinks (160G per rack of 10G servers).

Spine planes

Four independent "planes" of spine switches, each plane holding up to 48 spine switches. Every fabric switch in every pod connects to every spine switch in its plane → uniform, non-blocking connectivity between all pods in the building.

Edge pods

A flexible number of edge pods provide connectivity out of the building — up to 7.68 Tbps each to the backbone and inter-building fabrics.

Figure 1 — 2014 fabric: identical pods connect through 4 independent spine planes; edge pods reach the backbone.

3.2 Design decisions that mattered

DecisionWhat Meta didWhy it matters
Routing protocol Plain BGP4 only, minimal features; a centralized BGP controller can override any path. "Distributed control, centralized override" — simple, scalable, vendor-neutral.
Layer 3 everywhere All Layer-3 from TOR uplinks to edge; dual-stack IPv4/IPv6. No spanning-tree, no L2 domains; merchant silicon suffices.
Load balancing ECMP with flow-based hashing; 40G switch-to-switch links vs 10G server links. Statistically near-ideal distribution; elephant flows can't dominate.
Gradual scalability Pre-build physical skeleton; start at 4:1 oversubscription with 12 spines/plane, add spines to reach 1:1. Scale capacity by adding switches, not redesigning.
Physical layout Building designed around the fabric: BDF rooms at building center, identical straight fiber trunks, spine planes as failure domains. Network requirements positively shaped building design; turn-up time dropped dramatically.
Automation Top-down, fabric-level configuration; hardware disaggregated from tooling. Replace any component without changing software.
Takeaway The 2014 fabric replaced "big iron" with many small, identical units. Uniformity → programmability → automation. This is the philosophical foundation for everything that follows.

4. 2014–2018: FBOSS, Wedge & Fabric Aggregator

Era 2 · Owning the stack

Between the 2014 fabric and the 2019 redesign, Meta built its own hardware and software stack:

  • Wedge (2014) and Wedge 100 / 100S — Meta-designed top-of-rack switches.
  • FBOSS — Meta's own network operating system, giving a single software image across hardware platforms. Open-sourced and later central to the AI story.
  • Backpack (2016) — second-generation modular open switch, a 12-ASIC folded-Clos chassis delivering 128×100G.
  • Fabric Aggregator (2018) — a disaggregated layer to interconnect multiple data center buildings within a region, replacing full-mesh connections between fabrics. Built from Wedge 100S building blocks.
  • Express Backbone — the long-haul network interconnecting regions.

By 2019 the network served 2.6 billion people across 15 locations. But demand kept doubling, and two hard constraints appeared: power (regions already had 100+ MW facilities, and 400G optics/ASICs consume much more) and optics availability (400G optics couldn't be procured at Meta's scale yet).

5. 2019: F16, Minipack & HGRID

Era 3 · 100G at massive scale

The March 2019 post "Reinventing Facebook's data center network" announced a top-to-bottom redesign with three pillars: F16 (fabric topology), Minipack (building-block switch), and HGRID (regional aggregation).

5.1 The problem F16 solved

  • Racks needed 1.6 Tbps and more of inter-rack bandwidth.
  • Regions were growing from 3 buildings to 6 buildings — the old Fabric Aggregator approach wouldn't scale.
  • 400G optics were not available at the required volume, and 400G ASICs would blow the power budget.

5.2 F16 topology

The clever move: use the Broadcom Tomahawk 3 ASIC (which could do 32×400G or 128×100G) in its 128×100G mode, and build 16 single-chip planes instead of 4 multichip planes.

  • Each rack connects to 16 separate planes → 1.6T up / 1.6T down.
  • 16 × 128-port 100G fabric switches per pod (down from 48 multichip switches) → 3× fewer fabric chips.
  • Mature, proven 100G CWDM4-OCP optics — no dependence on bleeding-edge 400G.
  • Network is 2.25× flatter: same-fabric paths always 6 hops (was 6–12), building-to-building always 8 hops (was up to 24).
Figure 2 — 2019 F16 + HGRID: 16 planes of Minipack 100G fabric switches inside a building; HGRID stitches up to 6 buildings into a region.

5.3 Minipack: the universal building block

Minipack is a 128×100GE switch built around a single 12.8T Tomahawk 3 ASIC, replacing Backpack's 12-ASIC folded Clos. Key properties:

PropertyDetail
Power / space50% less than Backpack
Modularity8 hot-swappable Port Interface Modules (PIMs), orthogonal-direct chassis — pizza-box simplicity with chassis flexibility
PIM-16Q16×100G QSFP28, or 8×200G QSFP56, or 16×40G QSFP+ (backward compatible)
PIM-4DD4×400G QSFP-DD; mix-and-match with PIM-16Q in same chassis
Reverse gearboxesBridge TH3's 50G PAM4 SerDes lanes to 100G CWDM4's 25G NRZ optics
RolesSame box serves as fabric switch, spine, and aggregator

Meta contributed Minipack (built with Edgecore) and the jointly-developed Arista 7368X4 to OCP; both run FBOSS.

5.4 HGRID: scaling the region

HGRID evolved Fabric Aggregator using Minipack building blocks to interconnect up to 6 F16-equipped buildings. F16 also eliminated the old "edge pods" — fabric spine switches connect directly to HGRID — flattening East-West traffic and scaling regional uplinks to petabit levels.

Takeaway F16 shows a mature engineering judgment: rather than chase 400G/800G optics prematurely, Meta got the same 4× capacity from mature 100G optics + more parallelism (16 planes), while cutting power and hops. Design for the optics you can actually buy at scale.

6. 2020–2022: AI arrives

Era 4 · A new workload changes everything

AI didn't arrive as a single project — it crept in through short-form video recommendations in the late 2010s. Ranking content for Reels requires understanding every uploaded video and producing a personalized ranking per user — a problem orders of magnitude larger than ranking friends' posts. GPUs, which excel at vector/matrix math, became essential.

Meta's first AI clusters interconnected 4,000 GPUs for ranking and recommendation training. The company quickly realized AI infrastructure must be planned holistically across data center space, cooling, mechanical systems, hardware, network, storage, and software.

6.1 2022: The LLM shock

Large language models changed the scaling rules overnight:

  • Training job sizes jumped from 128 GPUs → 2,000 → 4,000 in a matter of weeks.
  • For the first time, thousands of GPUs had to run synchronously; a single straggler stalls the entire job.
  • Unlike web requests, you can't just retry on another machine — the whole cluster is one job. Failure handling became existential; Meta drove interruption rates down ~50× through industry collaboration.
Why AI breaks the old assumptions A web tier is a fleet of small, independent, retryable requests — you mask failures with software. An AI training cluster is a single distributed supercomputer: high-bandwidth, low-latency, tightly synchronized, and only as fast as its slowest GPU. The network stops being "plumbing" and becomes part of the computer.

7. 2024: Building Meta's GenAI Infrastructure

Era 4 · 24k-GPU clusters

In March 2024 Meta detailed its workhorse GenAI cluster design: two clusters of 24,576 NVIDIA H100 GPUs each, used to train Llama 3 and successors. The design deliberately explored two network technologies in parallel:

Cluster A — RoCE

RDMA over Converged Ethernet, built from Arista 7800 with Meta's Wedge400 and Minipack2 OCP rack switches. 400 Gbps endpoints.

Cluster B — InfiniBand

NVIDIA Quantum2 InfiniBand fabric, also 400 Gbps endpoints.

Both successfully trained large GenAI models (including Llama 3 on the RoCE cluster) without network bottlenecks, giving Meta a head-to-head comparison of interconnect technologies at scale.

7.1 The full stack

SoftwarePyTorch (Meta is largest contributor), NCCL, internal schedulerTopology-aware scheduling; process-group init reduced from hours to minutes

7.2 Performance lessons

  • Out-of-the-box large clusters had poor and inconsistent AllGather bandwidth (10–90% of ideal), while small clusters hit 90%+.
  • Fixes: topology-aware job scheduling (keep traffic in lower network layers), plus network routing co-optimized with NCCL.
  • Debuggability is a first-class problem at 24k GPUs: Meta built "desync debug" and a distributed collective flight recorder to find the one stalling GPU.
  • New techniques: FP8 training on H100, checkpointing thousands of ranks in hundreds of milliseconds.
Roadmap context By end of 2024 Meta targeted 350,000 H100 GPUs, with total compute equivalent to ~600,000 H100s. The two 24k clusters were just the template.

8. 2024: RoCE Networks at Scale

Era 4 · The backend network

The August 2024 SIGCOMM paper/blog "RoCE networks for distributed AI training at scale" is the definitive description of how Meta builds AI backend networks. Key architectural decisions:

8.1 Frontend / Backend separation

Training clusters run on two physically independent networks:

  • Frontend (FE): data ingestion, checkpointing, logging — uses the existing RSW→FSW hierarchy and the storage warehouse.
  • Backend (BE): the GPU-to-GPU training fabric — a specialized, non-blocking, lossless RoCEv2 network (RDMA encapsulated in UDP).

Separation lets the BE evolve, operate, and scale independently of the general-purpose DC network.

8.2 AI Zone topology

  • RTSW (rack training switch): leaf; scale-up connectivity within rack over copper DAC.
  • CTSW (cluster training switch): modular spine with deep buffers; scale-out across racks over 400G single-mode fiber.
  • ATSW (aggregator training switch): connects CTSWs across a DC building to extend the RoCE domain beyond one AI Zone.
  • Cross-AI-Zone links are oversubscribed by design; the job scheduler computes a "minimum cut" to place ranks and minimize cross-zone traffic.
Figure 3 — RoCE backend: GPUs → RTSW → CTSW form an AI Zone; ATSW connects zones. FE network runs separately for storage and control traffic.

8.3 Why AI traffic is hard

CharacteristicImplication
Low entropyFew, repetitive flows → standard ECMP hashing performs poorly
BurstinessOn/off behavior at millisecond scale
Elephant flowsEach burst can hit NIC line rate

8.4 The routing evolution

  1. Baseline ECMP: poor performance due to low flow entropy.
  2. Path pinning (route by destination RTSW downlink "slice"): worked when racks were fully allocated to one job, but fragmented placement + failures caused up to 30% degradation. Mitigated by 2× oversubscribed RTSW uplinks — effective but expensive.
  3. E-ECMP + Queue-Pair scaling: NCCL spreads collective traffic across multiple QPs; switches hash on the RoCE destination QP field (using ASIC UDF). Up to 40% improvement on AllReduce vs baseline ECMP.

8.5 Congestion control

  • DCQCN (the traditional RoCE congestion control) was disabled at 400G due to firmware bugs and poor CNP counting; Meta ran for over a year on PFC alone with stable performance.
  • Receiver-driven traffic admission: NCCL uses a two-stage copy; the sender may only post an RDMA write after a clear-to-send (CTS) from the receiver. This naturally bounds in-flight traffic.
  • Careful tuning of NCCL channel count / buffer size, plus high-priority switch queuing for CTS packets.
Takeaway For AI training, Meta treats the network, collective library (NCCL), and job scheduler as one co-designed system. The scheduler knows the topology; the NIC and switch hashing know the collective traffic; admission control is receiver-driven.

9. 2024: OCP — DSF & New Hardware

Era 5 · Open, disaggregated AI fabrics

At OCP Summit 2024, Meta unveiled the next generation of AI fabric building blocks.

9.1 DSF — Disaggregated Scheduled Fabric

DSF replaces monolithic chassis-based scheduled fabrics with a distributed system of leaf + spine boxes:

  • VoQ-based traffic scheduling → proactive congestion avoidance (vs. reactive signaling).
  • Runs on OCP-SAI + FBOSS; exposes standard Ethernet RoCE to endpoints (NVIDIA/AMD GPUs, Meta's own MTIA accelerators).
  • Arista 7700R4 series: DSF leaf (Broadcom Jericho3-AI, 18×800GE host ports, 14.4 Tbps, 16GB buffers) and DSF spine (Broadcom Ramon3, 128×800G fabric ports, 102.4 Tbps).

9.2 New 51T switches

Minipack3Broadcom Tomahawk551.2 Tbps, 64× OSFPMeta-designed, Celestica-manufactured; retimer-less; runs FBOSS
Cisco 8501Cisco Silicon One G20051.2 Tbps, 64× OSFPDesigned and manufactured by Cisco; runs FBOSS

Both are backward-compatible with 200G/400G and support upgrades to 400G/800G — the same multi-role, smooth-upgrade philosophy as the original Minipack.

9.3 Optics & NIC

  • 2×400G FR4 optics deployed for 400G/800G interconnection across backend, frontend, and DSF.
  • FBNIC: Meta's first in-house NIC ASIC — multi-host (up to 4 hosts), OCP NIC 3.0 compliant, upstreamed Linux driver, module designed by Marvell.

10. 2025: Gigawatt-Scale AI — NSF, Dual-Stage DSF, ESUN

Era 5 · Building-scale and beyond

10.1 The 129k-GPU statement

In late 2023/2024 Meta did something unprecedented: it emptied five production data center buildings and turned them into a single AI cluster with 129,000 H100 GPUs — built in months. This is only possible because of the region-based, fabric-first design philosophy established in 2014–2019.

10.2 OCP 2025 networking announcements

Dual-Stage DSF

DSF evolved to a 2-stage architecture supporting a non-blocking fabric of up to 18,432 xPUs — the fundamental building block for AI clusters that span entire data center buildings and even multiple regions.

NSF — Non-Scheduled Fabric

A parallel new architecture for the largest clusters (e.g. Prometheus, Meta's first gigawatt-scale AI supercluster):

  • Built entirely from shallow-buffer, disaggregated Ethernet switches
  • Low round-trip latency
  • Adaptive routing for load balancing and congestion avoidance
  • Three-tier topology for building-scale AI clusters

Minipack3N

New 51.2 Tbps OCP switch based on NVIDIA Spectrum-4, same system design as Minipack3, manufactured by Accton, running OCP-SAI + FBOSS. Expands Meta's multi-vendor silicon portfolio.

New optics

2×400G FR4 LITE (500m, cost-optimized for intra-DC) and 400G DR4 / 2×400G DR4 OSFP optics for AI host-side NIC and switch-side connectivity.

10.3 ESUN — Ethernet for Scale-Up Networking

Meta is a founding member of ESUN, an OCP Networking Project workstream to adapt Ethernet for scale-up domains (the ultra-high-bandwidth interconnect inside/next to AI accelerator racks — historically proprietary, e.g. NVLink). Members include AMD, Arista, ARM, Broadcom, Cisco, HPE, Marvell, Microsoft, NVIDIA, OpenAI, Oracle. Focus areas: protocol headers, error handling, lossless multi-hop topologies, in alignment with UEC and IEEE.

10.4 The rack is changing too

The 2025 "Infrastructure Evolution" post shows Meta's GB200-based Catalina rack: a 6-rack pod where 2 racks hold 72 NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs (~140 kW) and 4 racks are air-assisted liquid cooling (AALC) units — because Meta's traditional data centers lack facility liquid cooling. One such pod delivers ~360 PFLOPS FP16 while consuming 800× the power of a typical CPU pod. Data center design, power delivery, and cooling are now inseparable from network and compute design.

Where this is heading The trajectory is clear: from building-scale fabrics (2014) → region-scale fabrics (2019) → AI clusters that span multiple buildings and regions (2024–2025), with Ethernet (not proprietary interconnect) as the unifying technology, and power/cooling now the dominant constraint.

11. Key Design Principles

Across 11 years and multiple generations, a consistent philosophy emerges:

PrincipleHow Meta applies it
1. Disaggregate everythingReplace big proprietary chassis with many small identical units: pods (2014), single-ASIC Minipack planes (2019), DSF leaf/spine (2024).
2. Uniformity enables automationIdentical pods/planes → top-down, fabric-level configuration; hardware and tooling evolve independently.
3. Use mature technology at scaleF16 chose proven 100G CWDM4 optics over immature 400G; capacity came from parallelism (16 planes), not bleeding-edge links.
4. Own the software stackFBOSS gives one OS across generations of hardware and vendors (Broadcom, Cisco, NVIDIA silicon); OCP-SAI keeps it vendor-neutral.
5. Co-design network, software, and workloadTopology-aware schedulers, NCCL + E-ECMP/QP-scaling, receiver-driven admission — the network is part of the computer.
6. Design for graceful upgradesMinipack's swappable PIMs (40/100/200/400G), pre-built physical skeletons, backward-compatible 51T switches.
7. Separate concernsFrontend vs. backend networks; storage/checkpoint traffic isolated from GPU training fabric.
8. Open by defaultDesigns contributed to OCP; open SAI; FBOSS open-sourced; ESUN to standardize Ethernet scale-up.
9. Power and cooling are first-order constraintsF16 was shaped by DC power budget; 2025 racks need AALC liquid cooling; gigawatt-scale clusters (Prometheus) are planned around power availability.
