How Meta Designs Its Data Center

From the disaggregated network fabric of 2014, through the F16 / Minipack reinvention in 2019, to the gigawatt-scale AI clusters and custom silicon of the present — a tutorial built from Meta's own engineering posts.

The design philosophy that never changes

Meta's data center story is a story of recurring ideas reapplied as workloads changed from web traffic to AI training. Before the topologies, learn the five principles that connect every generation.

01

Disaggregate

Break big, specialized devices into many small, identical building blocks that can be procured from multiple vendors.

02

Open everything

Co-founded OCP in 2011; open hardware, FBOSS, and OCP-SAI let Meta (and everyone) buy commodity parts at scale.

03

Automate relentlessly

"Manage switches like servers." A single software image, fabric-level config, auto-remediation instead of human debugging.

04

Design for power & optics

Physical constraints (power budgets, optics availability) are treated as first-class inputs to every architecture decision.

05

Keep it simple, @scale

Choose mature tech now over bleeding-edge risk; favor fewer parts, fewer hops, fewer moving pieces — even at enormous scale.

Why this matters Machine-to-machine traffic inside Meta's data centers dwarfs user-facing traffic by "several orders of magnitude." Every design decision below exists to keep that internal network fast, cheap, and operable by small teams of engineers despite exponential growth.

2014 — The data center fabric

Meta's "next-generation" network turned the entire building into one flat, high-performance fabric instead of a hierarchy of oversubscribed clusters.

The problem with clusters

An old cluster was a big deployment unit: hundreds of cabinets aggregated on a few large, high-radix cluster switches. It had hard limits:

  • Port density caps size. Bigger clusters need bigger switches, available only from a handful of vendors — and the "XXL" bleeding-edge boxes have proprietary internals that need specialized expertise.
  • Oversubscription by assumption. Inter-cluster bandwidth was much lower than intra-cluster, which forced applications to stay inside one cluster — exactly what distributed apps don't want.
  • Failure blast radius. Large areas depend on a few boxes; hardware or software failure is a big deal.
  • Endless balancing. The tension between cluster size, rack bandwidth, and out-of-cluster bandwidth never resolves itself.

The fix: make the entire building one network, built from small, identical units instead of a handful of giant devices.

The fabric model: pods and planes

The core idea is disaggregation into identical building blocks. The network is composed of "pods" (48-rack units, each served by four fabric switches) and four independent planes of spine switches (each scalable to 48 devices). Every fabric switch in every pod connects to every spine switch in its local plane.

Design decisions worth copying

  • One protocol, one control philosophy. Plain BGP4 for everything, plus a centralized controller that can override paths by pure software — "distributed control, centralized override." Heavy use of ECMP with flow hashing for near-ideal load distribution.
  • Plan for scale, not for day-one. Pre-build all passive infrastructure (the "skeleton"), start at 4:1 oversubscription with 12 of 48 spines, and grow to 2:1 or 1:1 by adding spine devices — no redesign required.
  • Physical layout is part of the design. Spine and edge live in central BDF rooms, pre-equipped early; data halls attach identically. Long "straight-line" trunks keep cabling simple; each spine plane is one failure domain you can take out of service safely.
  • Automate above the device. Config is defined at the fabric level and rendered down to each box; devices self-discover their role; monitoring keys on baselines/outliers with auto-remediation. "The overall behavior is more important than an individual box or link."
  • Backward compatibility via "virtual clusters." Clusters survive as logical named areas over the fabric, so the rest of the stack is untouched.

2019 — F16 and Minipack

Four years on, the fabric served 2.6 billion people across 15 regions. To quadruple capacity without being crushed by power and optics constraints, Meta reworked the topology and the switch itself.

Two pressures, two constraints

Demand demanded higher per-rack speeds (1.6T and beyond) and bigger regions (from 3 to 6 buildings each). But the network could not eat a disproportionate share of a region's power, and the industry couldn't supply 400G optics at Meta's scale and schedule.

The pivot: instead of chasing 400G links, use mature 100G CWDM4-OCP optics to get the same 4× capacity — by splitting into more, smaller planes.

F4 → F16: same capacity, radically fewer chips

Both paths use Broadcom's Tomahawk 3 (TH3) ASIC. A "F4" design would need 4 multichip 400G fabric switches (48 ASICs per pod); the F16 design instead builds 16 single-chip 100G planes with just 16 ASICs per pod — 3× fewer fabric chips and control planes, at half the power of the previous Backpack switch.

Minipack: one ASIC, many roles

Minipack is a 128-port 100G switch on a single 12.8T ASIC, using half the power and space of the multichip Backpack — yet it keeps a chassis's flexibility through swappable Port Interface Modules (PIMs): PIM-16Q supports 16×100G, or in retimer mode 8×200G, or 16×40G; PIM-4DD supports 4×400G. The same box can serve as fabric switch, spine, or aggregator, and can be upgraded speed-by-speed as optics mature.

  • MiniLake control module: a custom COM-Express microserver so switches are managed "like servers."
  • Reverse gearboxes: bridge TH3's 50G PAM4 SerDes to the mature 25G-NRZ 100G optics.
  • HGRID: the aggregation layer scales regions to six F16-equipped buildings, flattening East-West traffic.
  • Second source: the jointly-designed Arista 7368X4 offers a redundant supply chain; both run FBOSS and are contributed to OCP.
The pattern repeats "We contribute the complete design package to OCP," "one single-chip design that fulfills multiple roles," "manage switches like servers," "early deployment, stay in production, iterate." These are the same principles from 2014, applied to the switch hardware itself.

2020–2023 — The AI inflection

AI workloads broke the assumptions of a decade. A CPU is a load–store machine; a GPU is a vector/matrix machine doing orders of magnitude more math. That changes how a data center is built.

From "mask failures" to "keep the job alive"

Web workloads scale by buying cheap, unreliable CPUs and masking failures with software. AI is different: one training job runs across thousands of GPUs synchronously, and any single straggler or failure halts the whole job. Meta's initial AI clusters interconnected 4,000 GPUs; with LLMs, jobs grew from 128 GPUs to 2,000 to 4,000 within weeks. Through industry collaboration Meta drove the training interruption rate down ~50×.

Grand Teton and the 24k H100 clusters

Announced at OCP 2022, Grand Teton is Meta's in-house GPU platform: a monolithic chassis integrating power, control, compute, and fabric interfaces, with 8 GPUs and a 1:1 GPU-to-RDMA-NIC mapping, contributed to OCP. In late 2023 Meta built two 24,576-GPU H100 clusters — one on RoCE, one on InfiniBand — each sized to consume all the power available in a building (typically low tens of megawatts). They trained Llama 3.

Cluster 1 — RoCE

RDMA over Converged Ethernet built on Arista 7800 with Wedge400 and Minipack2 OCP rack switches.

Cluster 2 — InfiniBand

NVIDIA Quantum2 InfiniBand fabric. Both interconnect 400G endpoints.

Engineering around a tight power budget

Hardware lead times are long, so Meta adapted: it modified Grand Teton to raise GPU TDP to 700W and moved to HBM3 — but had to stay in the existing air-cooled environment, forcing mechanical and thermal redesign and re-validation. It also reorganized the data hall: moving supporting "reader" services out, packing as many GPU racks as possible to maximize compute density and network size.

The power-limited design rule Data centers are increasingly power-constrained, not space-constrained. Cluster size is now set by "all the power available in a building," not by network port density — the inverse of the 2014 problem.

2024–2026 — AI at massive scale

The trajectory kept steepening: a 129k-GPU single cluster, rack power past 100 kW, custom silicon, and the first gigawatt AI cluster.

Largest single training cluster at Meta (GPU count) — the scale-up story

129,000 GPUs in one cluster

To scale another order of magnitude, Meta did something unprecedented: it emptied five production data centers and built a single 129k-H100 cluster in months. (By end of 2024 the fleet target was 350,000 H100s — roughly 600,000 H100-equivalent of compute.) Llama 3.1 405B alone trained across more than 16,000 H100s on 15 trillion tokens.

Catalina racks and the power wall

At OCP 2024 Meta unveiled Catalina, a high-power rack built around NVIDIA's GB200 Grace Blackwell superchip and the new ORv3 spec supporting up to 140 kW. A Catalina pod is six racks: two compute racks with 72 Blackwell GPUs (~140 kW) plus four Air-Assisted Liquid Cooling (AALC) racks, because legacy data halls have no facility liquid cooling. The pod yields ~360 PFLOPS of FP16 — consuming over 800× the power of a typical CPU while producing hundreds of thousands of times the compute.

Custom silicon: MTIA

MTIA (Meta Training and Inference Accelerator) is Meta's family of custom chips. MTIA v1 arrived in 2023; MTIA v2 (2024) is optimized for ranking and recommendation inference, is deployed at scale for ads workloads, and delivers large efficiency gains over vendor silicon. A training chip for ranking/recommendations is ramping, with more chips in development.

Heterogeneity is deliberate — Meta runs AMD MI300X, H200, B200, GB200, and MTIA together — and it's why open standards (PyTorch, Triton, OCP) matter: a consistent programming interface lets engineers target any accelerator.

Where the silicon roadmap is heading

  • Advanced packaging: with reticle size capped at 830 mm², scaling means chiplets via 2.5D/3D packaging — new cooling and efficiency challenges.
  • Memory disaggregation: reasoning models and test-time inference pressure memory; expect HBM stacked by chiplets, and possibly off-chip/off-machine memory.
  • Silicon photonics: the path beyond 3.2T of "silicon shoreline" and away from power-hungry backplanes, trading power for range and scale.

AI networking: scale-out and scale-up

AI clusters need a dedicated, isolated backend network that connects every GPU at high bandwidth and low, bounded latency. This is where Meta's fabric lineage most directly meets the AI era.

The AI Zone fabric

For training, each cluster runs two independent networks: a frontend (FE) for data ingestion and checkpointing, and a backend (BE) for training traffic. The backend is a two-stage Clos "AI Zone": RTSW rack training switches (scale-up within the rack over copper DAC) → CTSW cluster training switches (scale-out, deep buffers) → ATSW aggregation across zones. The transport for most AI capacity is RoCEv2.

DSF — Disaggregated Schedule Fabric

  • Mechanism: VOQ (virtual output queue) based scheduling, powered by open OCP-SAI + FBOSS.
  • Best for: scale-out interconnect across entire buildings; a full-size DSF L2 zone is ~18k 800G GPUs.
  • Scale: evolved to dual-stage, non-blocking, up to 18,432 XPUs.
  • Endpoints: Ethernet-based RoCE to XPUs/NICs, including Meta's MTIA.

NSF — Non-Scheduled Fabric

  • Mechanism: shallow-buffer, disaggregated Ethernet switches.
  • Best for: low round-trip latency, adaptive routing, the foundation for gigawatt-scale clusters like Prometheus.
  • Scale: three-tier (RTSW/FTSW/STSW) with ECMP; typical oversubscription L2→BAG ≈ 4.5:1.
  • Endpoints: 400G/800G Ethernet NICs on GB300 and similar accelerators.

BAG: connecting regions into a gigawatt cluster

Even a full building isn't enough. Backend Aggregation (BAG) is a centralized Ethernet "super spine" that stitches DSF and NSF fabrics together across data center buildings and regions — enabling mega-clusters. In Prometheus, Meta's 1-gigawatt AI cluster spanning 5+ buildings (plus weatherproof tents and colo facilities) and tens of thousands of GPUs, BAG interconnects regional fabrics with petabit-scale capacity (16–48 Pbps per region pair).

  • Hardware: modular chassis with Jericho3 line cards (up to 432×800G ports); deep buffers to support lossless congestion control (PFC) over long inter-BAG cables.
  • Routing: eBGP with link-bandwidth attributes and Unequal-Cost Multipath (UCMP); MACsec protects BAG-to-BAG links.
  • Topology: planar (one-to-one, simpler) or spread (distributed links, more resilient) connections between regions.
  • Resilience: failure domains analyzed at BAG, data hall, and power-distribution levels; planes drained and routes aggregated to avoid blackholing.

Storage and software: the rest of the stack

A data center is more than switches and GPUs. Storage and the control plane are where AI's "data-hungry" nature and Meta's software-first culture show up.

Storage: "if AI is the brain, storage is the memory"

Meta runs hundreds of exabyte-scale storage clusters under a horizontally-scalable block layer called Tectonic. For AI, the old metadata-heavy BLOB stack (with global lookups that could take hundreds of milliseconds) was a bottleneck — a single slow storage fetch can stall every GPU in a synchronized training step.

  • Unified metadata schema backed by ZippyDB collapses multi-layer lookups into O(1) path resolution.
  • No data-plane proxy: a fat client SDK streams bytes directly from storage servers to GPUs, cutting latency and power.
  • Regional deployment: lean BLOB storage is colocated with GPUs in every AI region, since power spent on storage is power not spent on GPUs.
  • For training, a home-grown FUSE API over Tectonic (optimized for flash) plus a co-developed Hammerspace parallel NFS let thousands of GPUs checkpoint synchronously and debug interactively.

The throughline

Look past the acronyms and the same five moves keep reappearing — that's what makes Meta's approach recognizable across fifteen years.
